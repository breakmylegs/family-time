## In short

Your best guess of your own screen time sits beside {child}'s, and the family takes on one challenge together each week, such as screen-free tea. Nobody earns points from it and nothing is lost if you miss it.

## How to use it

1. Each week, on the Family screen, tap Change beside Your screen time this week and give your best guess of your own leisure screen time, not work. Nobody checks it.
2. Tap Set one under The whole family, name the challenge and choose how many times, for example Screen-free tea, 5 times. Agree it with {child} first.
3. Whenever the family does it, anyone taps We did it: you here, a grandparent, or {child} on the kiosk. The bar fills up to the target and stops there.
4. A challenge lasts until the week resets, so set a new one each week. Look at both at the family review.

## Why it helps

In one trial, when every member of the family cut back on leisure screens for two weeks, children gained about 45 minutes a day of active leisure. A study that followed families over time linked parents' device use during time with their child to more difficult behaviour, and the child's difficult behaviour to more parent device use. Cutting back together makes it something the family does, not something done to {child}.

## What {child} sees

The challenge with its bar and a We did it button, and the grown-ups' screen time this week: the total of the parents who have given theirs.

## Watch out for

Turning it into a contest between parents, or between {child} and you. It is shared progress only. If the numbers start an argument, leave yours blank for a week and keep the challenge.

## The research

Schmidt-Persson and colleagues, 2024, the SCREENS trial. Whole families with children aged 4 to 17 cut their leisure screen use for two weeks; children gained about 45 minutes a day of active leisure and had better scores for emotional and behavioural difficulties. McDaniel and Radesky, 2018, followed parents and children over time: parents' device use during time together was linked to more acting-out behaviour later, and the child's behaviour was linked to more parent device use in turn.
